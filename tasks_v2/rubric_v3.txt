**Rubric: how much a single text is worth to its reader**

1. The text commits to the one or two points the writer thinks actually matter and leaves the rest out, rather than giving an even, complete tour of the standard reasons, mechanisms or factors.
2. It tells the reader something a careful reader of a reference summary would not already have, such as a correction, a non-obvious consequence, a real case, or a judgment call; accurate textbook coverage alone earns nothing here.
3. A smooth step-by-step walk through a mechanism ("this causes… which means… so… on top of that…", "добавьте сюда…") counts as a list in disguise and weighs against the text, however naturally it is joined.
4. Practical, checkable specifics such as procedures, legal limits, how often something is free, how a device is controlled, or what a body does at a given threshold must be correct, and one confidently wrong specific outweighs any amount of fluent explanation.
5. Precise-sounding ranges and figures ("10 to 50 ms", "blacks out around 4 or 5", "every few days") count against the text unless the writer shows where they come from or clearly marks them as rough.
6. A first-person anecdote that neatly illustrates the writer's thesis ("I knew one company that…"), with no checkable particulars and no complication, counts as invented color and weighs against the text.
7. Real experience or professional knowledge, where present, is particular and slightly inconvenient, such as a specific job, place, incident or mistake, and it changes what the answer says rather than serving as a setup or a punchline.
8. Performed casualness does not count as a personal voice: friendly openers, "haha", "to be fair", staccato asides ("Pretty simple and pretty bad."), scare quotes and joke sign-offs are decoration, not evidence that a real person knows the subject.
9. When the topic involves a legend, an anecdote, or a disputed or poorly documented story, the writer flags what is documented and what is retold instead of narrating it as settled fact.
10. Guesses are marked as guesses and gaps are admitted plainly, without blanket hedging or a disclaimer.
11. When the question's premise is wrong or loaded, the text says so and gives the correct picture briefly, without turning the correction into a crisp "not X but Y" or "they don't need to be A; they need to be B" slogan.
12. Each sentence adds something new, and restating a mechanism, or recasting it as a polished contrast, analogy or quotable one-liner ("The form is the whole point."), is padding.
13. Technical terms appear only when they do real work and are tied to something the reader already knows, not to signal expertise.
14. It speaks to the asker as a capable adult (or to a child appropriately), without lecturing, reassuring, or adding unrequested offers and advice, and it stops when the point is made, with no summary line, "together these…" wrap-up, verdict or neat moral.
15. Judge the text by whether a reader could trust it and act on it as coming from someone who knows this, not by how accurate-sounding, well-structured, friendly or comprehensive it looks, because polish, warmth and coverage are never evidence of quality.

**Scale**

- **2:** Off-topic, wrong or lazy, or a polished piece resting on a confidently wrong practical specific, an invented illustrative anecdote, or a legend told as fact.
- **5:** Accurate and fluent but standard. It is an even mechanism walkthrough or tour of the usual points, possibly dressed in a chatty persona, with illustrative figures, smooth contrasts or a wrap-up line. Any text matching this scores at most 5, however friendly or polished.
- **8:** A real person's selective answer with a clear take or a non-obvious point. Its specifics are correct or honestly hedged, and it has no performed persona, at most minor padding.
- **10:** Clearly someone who knows this, answering this exact question. It is selective, correct on the details a reader would act on, candid about what is known and what is legend, concrete only where it has grounds, and it stops when done.
