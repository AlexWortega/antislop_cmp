A simple rain gauge collects rain falling into an opening of known area and reports the result as a depth, usually in millimetres or inches. That depth shows how much water would have covered flat ground if none had drained, evaporated, or soaked in. Many gauges use a funnel with a wide opening that feeds a narrower measuring tube. Because the same volume of water rises higher in a narrow tube, small amounts of rain become easier to read precisely. The tube's scale is marked to account for the area ratio, so readings still give true rainfall depth. Wind is a common source of error: gusts around the gauge can carry raindrops past the opening, causing undercounting. Evaporation or splashing before reading can also distort results.
