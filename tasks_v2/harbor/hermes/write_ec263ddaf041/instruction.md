A simple rain gauge measures rainfall by catching rain through a circular opening of known area. The water is funneled into a measuring tube marked in depth units, such as millimeters or inches. The reading tells how deep the rain would have been if it had fallen evenly over the ground, with no runoff or soaking in.

The opening is often wider than the measuring tube. This concentrates the collected water into a narrower column, making small amounts of rain easier to read accurately. The scale on the tube is adjusted for the difference in area, so the reading still represents actual rainfall depth.

Two common causes of inaccurate readings are poor placement and evaporation. A gauge near trees, buildings, or fences may collect too little or too much because of sheltering or splashing. If the water is not read soon after the storm, some may evaporate.
