A siphon transfers water between two containers by utilizing gravity and atmospheric pressure to move liquid over an elevated barrier. The process begins when the tube is filled with water, creating a continuous column. Gravity pulls the water down the longer, descending leg of the tube toward the lower container. This downward movement creates a partial vacuum or reduced pressure at the top of the siphon. Atmospheric pressure pushing on the surface of the source water then forces more liquid up the shorter leg to fill this low-pressure zone, sustaining the flow.

The outlet must be positioned lower than the source water surface because the driving force is the weight difference between the water columns in the two legs. If the outlet were higher or level with the source, gravity would not pull the water down with sufficient force to overcome the upward lift required on the intake side. The net gravitational pull only exists when the exiting column is heavier than the rising column.

Air entering the tube interrupts this process by breaking the continuous liquid column. When air bubbles enter, they disrupt the cohesive chain of water molecules necessary for transmitting the suction effect. This break equalizes the pressure inside the tube with the outside atmosphere, stopping the pressure differential that drives the flow. Consequently, the siphon fails until the tube is re-primed and filled entirely with liquid again.
