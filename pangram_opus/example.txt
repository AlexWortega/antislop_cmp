Aluminum conducts heat better than cast iron, meaning heat moves through it faster. That is why a thin aluminum pan heats up quickly and responds fast when you raise or lower the burner. But staying hot is not just about conductivity. It also depends on how much heat the pan can store.

A cast-iron pan is usually much thicker and heavier than a thin aluminum pan. Because it has more mass, it can hold a larger amount of heat energy. Once it is hot, adding cold food does not cool it down as quickly. The thin aluminum pan may transfer heat efficiently, but it has less stored heat to give.

For example, if you sear a cold steak, a hot cast-iron skillet often keeps sizzling after the steak hits the surface. A thin aluminum pan may lose temperature quickly, so the steak can steam or gray before browning well.

One limitation of the comparison is that pan design matters. A thick aluminum pan, especially one with a heavy base, can hold heat much better than a very thin one. So the difference is not only the metal, but also the pan’s thickness, weight, and construction.
