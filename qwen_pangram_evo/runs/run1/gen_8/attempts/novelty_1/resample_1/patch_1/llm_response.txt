The context programs show two separate failure modes.

1. **Empty outputs.** Long rule lists make the model plan and check every rule, so it burns its whole reasoning budget. The current program's big checklist produced 8 empty outputs.
2. **AI flags.** Even with "casual" instructions, the outputs read as tidy explanatory prose: an ordered mechanism, balanced clauses and full textbook-style coverage. Swapping in a few casual words ("Honestly,") did not change that underlying shape.

The only pass came from a first-person, run-on, detail-driven rewrite. That suggests the detector rewards loose, conversational syntax more than vocabulary tweaks.

The new approach does not ban patterns one by one. It gives the model one compact, imitable target voice: a forum regular typing on a phone. That voice brings loose punctuation, run-on clauses joined with "and"/"so", occasional "tbh/imo", sentences starting with "And" or "But", hedges, and an abrupt end. Ordered explanation is broken on purpose: lead with the gut answer, mention the mechanism the way you'd half-remember it, and drift a bit.

Thinking is capped hard at one or two lines, and the prompt says outright that it contains no checklist to verify. Keeping the prompt short also cuts the urge to self-audit. Task-fidelity rules (no invented facts, length, emails, rewrites) are packed into a few lines so they still hold the task score.
